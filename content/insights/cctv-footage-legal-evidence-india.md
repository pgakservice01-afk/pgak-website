**Straight answer: yes, CCTV footage can be admitted as evidence in Indian courts, but only if you can show it's authentic — an unaltered recording, an accurate timestamp, and a clear chain of custody, usually backed by a certificate under the law governing electronic records. This is general guidance, not legal advice; verify the specifics of your situation with a lawyer or compliance advisor.**

A theft happens, or a dispute lands on your desk, and the first instinct is relief: "we have it on camera." The footage existing and the footage being usable in court are two different things, and the gap between them is where most site owners get caught out.

## Is CCTV footage actually admissible in India?

Yes, as a general matter. Electronic records, including video, are recognised as evidence in Indian courts. What's not automatic is that a court accepts your footage without question — like any evidence, it can be challenged, and video is challenged specifically on whether it's the real, unedited recording from the device it claims to be from.

That distinction — admissible in principle, but contestable on authenticity — is the one thing every site owner should internalise. Having the clip is step one. Being able to show nobody touched it is step two, and step two is the one people skip.

## What actually makes footage hold up?

Three things, in practice.

**An intact original.** The recording as it came off the DVR or NVR, not a re-encoded or trimmed clip. If you cut a video down to "just the relevant bit" before saving it, you've handed the other side an easy argument that something was removed — even if nothing was.

**An accurate timestamp.** Cameras that have drifted out of sync with real time, or recorders where the clock was never set correctly after a power cut, produce footage that's hard to tie to the reported time of the incident. This is a cheap thing to check and an expensive thing to discover wrong later.

**A documented chain of custody.** Who exported the footage, when, onto what device, and who has had access to that copy since. A clip that travelled from the recorder to someone's phone to a WhatsApp group before reaching you has a custody trail nobody can reconstruct.

## Does it need a certificate?

Generally, yes — electronic evidence in India needs to be accompanied by a certificate that speaks to how the recording was produced and that it's a faithful copy, under the law governing electronic records. Exactly how that certificate is worded and presented can differ by court and by the specifics of the case, which is precisely the part worth checking with a legal advisor before you're standing in front of one. Don't treat any format found online as a template to copy blind.

## What should you actually do, practically?

**Export immediately, once you know there's an incident.** Normal storage cycles overwrite footage on a schedule, and disputes often surface days after the event — a wage dispute, an insurance claim, a police complaint filed late. If you know footage matters, get it off the rolling storage the same day, onto a separate, logged export.

**Keep the original file format.** Don't screen-record a playback or crop the clip for size. Export using the recorder's own export function, which preserves metadata a screen-recording strips out.

**Log who accessed it.** A simple note — date, name, purpose — each time the export is copied or viewed is cheap insurance against a custody challenge later.

**Check your camera clocks periodically.** A drifted timestamp is invisible day to day and only matters the one day it's tested against a police report or a punch-clock record.

## Where this doesn't help you

A certificate and a clean export don't make weak footage strong. If the camera was too far from the incident to identify anyone, or the recording quality was too poor to make out what happened, no amount of proper custody fixes that. The legal process protects footage that's genuinely useful — it can't manufacture detail that was never captured. That's a camera-placement and resolution problem, not a paperwork one, and it's worth solving before an incident, not after.

None of this is a substitute for legal advice on an actual case — rules on evidence procedure and how courts expect certificates presented can vary, so involve a lawyer or compliance advisor early rather than after the footage is needed.
