**Straight answer: video smoke and flame detection can complement a certified fire detection system by giving an earlier visual cue in places where smoke is slow to reach ceiling detectors, such as high-bay warehouses, open yards and stockpiles. It does not replace the fire detection and alarm system your building needs, and it should never be the reason any required detector, alarm, extinguisher or fire watch is removed.**

Factory owners sometimes ask whether the cameras they already own could "also do fire". It is a fair question, because some specialist video analytics do look for visible patterns of smoke and flame. The honest answer is that this is a specialist design decision involving a qualified fire professional, not a feature to switch on, and that whatever a camera adds sits on top of the required system.

PGAK has no published evidence record for smoke or flame detection. Where it is offered, it is a specialist capability evaluated per site. PGAK's [hot-work recording](/industrial-cctv) is a different thing: it flags an activity beside flammable material, and is explicitly not fire detection.

## How does a certified alarm differ from a camera?

| | Certified fire detection and alarm system | Video smoke and flame analytics |
|---|---|---|
| What it senses | Smoke particles, heat or flame radiation at the detector | Visible patterns in a camera image |
| Designed by | A qualified fire-system designer, to the codes and standards that apply to your premises | Specialist analytics configured per camera view |
| Works in the dark or in smoke-filled rooms | Yes, within its design | Only if the camera can still see a usable image |
| Raises the building alarm | Yes, that is its job | Only if specifically integrated and approved for that role; do not assume it |
| Maintenance and testing | Under the fire system's maintenance regime | Under the video system's support terms |
| Role | Required protection | An additional view a person checks |

The point of the table is not that cameras are worse. They sense different things, and they fail in different ways. A ceiling smoke detector in a 12-metre-high bay may respond late because smoke stratifies below the roof. A camera may see a smoke plume at floor level earlier, but only if it is pointing that way, has light, and is not looking at steam from a boiler vent.

## Where can video add something?

- **High-bay and large-volume buildings**, where a camera with a clear view of the floor may see smoke before it reaches the ceiling.
- **Outdoor storage**: stockpiles of husk, bales, scrap, timber or packaging, where ceiling detectors do not exist.
- **Areas already watched by a camera for other reasons**, such as a yard or a loading apron.
- **Verification**, so that when a fire alarm sounds, a person can look at the relevant camera before or while responding.

## Where does it add little or nothing?

- **Small rooms with working detectors.** The detector will usually respond first.
- **Dark areas** without enough light for the camera to see smoke.
- **Areas full of steam, dust or exhaust** as part of normal work, such as dryers, boiler houses or diesel bays, where false alerts will be frequent unless carefully tuned.
- **Anywhere it would be used as a reason to cut required protection.** That is not a trade-off to make.

## The vendor-supported test checklist

Use this before buying, with the vendor and a qualified fire professional present. It is the original asset of this article.

**Before the test**

- [ ] The vendor names the exact product, version and the camera models it supports.
- [ ] The vendor states, in writing, the role it is designed for: additional detection, verification, or an approved detection device. Get the approval documents if they claim the last.
- [ ] A qualified fire professional has agreed that adding video does not change the required system.
- [ ] The test method is the manufacturer's approved one. No fires are lit and no material is burned to "test the camera".
- [ ] Each camera view is listed with its height, distance to the area watched, and lighting by time of day.

**During the test**

- [ ] The approved test is run in each camera view, by day and at night.
- [ ] The time from the test starting to the alert appearing is noted.
- [ ] The alert reaches a named person, on the device they actually carry.
- [ ] Normal sources of confusion are run deliberately: steam, dust from loading, vehicle exhaust, headlights, sunlight reflections, a welding job with a permit.

**After the test**

- [ ] Every alert, true or false, is listed with its clip.
- [ ] False alerts per camera per day are estimated from a normal working week, not a quiet demonstration.
- [ ] The response steps are written: who looks, what they check, when they raise the building alarm by the normal means.
- [ ] The required fire system's maintenance and testing schedule is unchanged.

### Illustrative example

These numbers are invented and round, to show the arithmetic only.

Suppose a pilot runs on 4 yard cameras for 2 weeks (14 days).

- Approved tests run: 8 (each camera, day and night). Tests that produced an alert: 6. Two night tests on the darkest camera were missed.
- False alerts in normal work: 70 over 14 days on 4 cameras = 70 ÷ (14 × 4) = 1.25 per camera per day, mostly from dust when lorries arrived.

That tells you the darkest camera cannot do this job without lighting, and that dust from lorries needs tuning before anyone will keep reading the alerts. It does not tell you the system is "accurate", and it says nothing at all about whether your required fire system is adequate. That is a separate question for your fire professional.

## Questions to ask about alarms and response

- Who is the qualified person responsible for your required fire detection and alarm system, and have they seen the video proposal?
- Is the video alert meant to raise the building alarm? If so, under whose approval?
- If the network or the processing unit fails, what happens? The required system must not depend on either.
- Who looks at a video alert at 3 a.m., and how quickly?
- How are false alerts reviewed, so they do not train people to ignore all alerts? [Why alerts get muted](/insights/ai-cctv-false-alarms-how-to-reduce) explains the pattern.
