**Straight answer: proxy attendance lasts where the system records a credential rather than a person, and where manual entries are routine. Reduce it by keeping evidence with each record, cutting manual entries, covering every entrance and reconciling against a second source. Then make sure every suspected case is reviewed by a person, explained to the employee and open to appeal, because a flag from a machine is not proof and must never trigger a pay deduction on its own.**

Most operations managers know proxy attendance happens on their site. Few know how much, because the systems they use were not designed to show it. The goal of this article is to make it visible and handle it fairly, not to promise it disappears.

## Why does proxy attendance survive most systems?

**Registers** record a signature, often signed in batches.

**Cards and PINs** record that a valid card was shown or a PIN typed. Both can be handed to a colleague.

**Fingerprint machines** are harder to fool, but they grow a fallback. When worn or wet fingers will not read, a supervisor enters attendance manually, or a PIN is issued. Once that is routine, the fallback is the weak point. See [why fingerprint attendance fails at industrial gates](/insights/fingerprint-attendance-system-why-it-fails).

**Face recognition** removes the card and the PIN, but it has exceptions too: faces it cannot identify, people who are not enrolled, and the question of whether it rejects a printed photo or a phone screen. Test that on your own gate; see [can someone fool face attendance with a photo](/insights/can-a-photo-fool-face-attendance).

The pattern is the same in every case: proxy attendance moves to wherever exceptions are handled loosely.

## Which controls actually reduce it?

**1. Keep evidence with the record.** If a disputed entry has a snapshot, a gate and a time attached, a reviewer can check it instead of relying on memory. If your system stores nothing but an ID and a timestamp, a dispute comes down to one person's word against another's.

**2. Make manual entries rare and accountable.** Count last month's manual entries as a share of all entries, by shift and by supervisor. Use that as your baseline. Every manual entry should carry a reason and the name of the approver, and the approver should not be the person who made the entry.

**3. Cover every entrance.** A system on the main gate measures the people who use the main gate. Side gates, loading bays and canteen exits are where mid-shift absence hides.

**4. Reconcile against a second source.** Production output, contractor rosters, canteen counts or vehicle logs. When two independent records consistently disagree for one team or shift, that is where to look.

**5. Review exceptions on a fixed rhythm.** A daily review of a short list is more effective than a monthly hunt through thousands of records.

## The human-reviewed exception log

This is the core of the article: a log format that makes proxy attendance visible while protecting people from wrong accusations. Use it whatever system you run.

| Field | What to record | Example (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|
| Exception ID | Unique, never reused | EX-1042 |
| Date, time, gate | From the system | 3 Mar, 06:04, Gate 2 |
| Recorded identity | Whose attendance was marked | Emp ref 0317 |
| Exception type | Manual entry · credential used without biometric · unmatched face · same person at two gates · present but not rostered · roster says present, no record | Manual entry |
| Evidence attached | Snapshot, device log, roster line, supervisor note | Snapshot, supervisor note |
| Reviewer | A named person, trained, not the supervisor who made the entry | HR officer A |
| Reviewer's finding | No issue · data error corrected · needs conversation · referred to manager | Needs conversation |
| Employee informed | Date and how | 4 Mar, in person |
| Employee's explanation | In their words | "Finger would not read; supervisor entered me" |
| Decision and decider | A second named person decides; reason in one line | Accepted; enrolment to be redone |
| Appeal requested? | Yes or no, date | No |
| Appeal outcome | Decided by someone senior to the decider | — |
| Record change | What was corrected, by whom; original kept | Re-enrolment booked |

### Audit trail rules

- **Nothing is deleted or overwritten.** A correction is a new entry that points to the old one.
- **Every change has a who, when and why.**
- **The reviewer and the decider are different people.**
- **Access is limited** to named roles, and viewing a snapshot is itself logged.
- **The log is kept** for the period your record-keeping rules require, and snapshots are deleted on the retention schedule you have published. See [attendance data: what to keep and what to delete](/insights/attendance-data-retention-what-to-delete).

### The appeal workflow

1. The employee is told what was flagged and shown the evidence, in a language they read.
2. They can explain within a stated number of working days, with a colleague or representative present if they wish.
3. The decider records a decision and a reason.
4. The employee can ask for a review by someone more senior, who did not take part in the first decision.
5. Until the appeal is decided, the attendance record shows "under review", not "absent".

## Why must a flag never trigger a deduction on its own?

Because every system makes mistakes, and an automatic penalty turns a technical error into an injustice.

There is also a legal reason. The **Code on Wages, 2019** (No. 29 of 2019; India Code text as on 21 November 2025) says in section 18(1) that no deductions may be made from wages except those it authorises. Section 19 says a fine may be imposed only for acts and omissions specified in a notice approved in advance by the appropriate government or prescribed authority, only after the employee has had an opportunity to show cause, and the total fines in a wage period cannot exceed three per cent of the wages payable for it. Section 20 limits deductions for absence to the proportion of the period the person was actually absent.

How a proxy-attendance finding should be handled — as a correction of attendance, as a fine, or under your disciplinary procedure — depends on your standing orders and the rules applicable to your establishment. <!-- UNRESOLVED: Punjab rules on fines/deductions under Code on Wages; standing orders under IR Code --> Take advice before acting on any finding. What the law makes plain is that a system output alone is not a basis for docking pay.

This is general information, not legal advice. Confirm your own obligations with a qualified adviser.

## Where does face recognition help, and where does it not?

Face recognition on a suitable gate camera removes the card and PIN, can attach a snapshot to each event, and can cover more than one gate if those cameras see faces at a usable size. Those properties help the controls above.

It does not remove exceptions. Poor placement, backlight, coverings and crowds all cause misses, and misses create pressure for manual entries. Face-recognition attendance has no published PGAK evidence record yet, so treat it as something to evaluate on your own gate, alongside your current system. The [face recognition](/features/face-recognition) page explains what is checked first, and [what affects face recognition accuracy](/insights/face-recognition-accuracy-what-affects-it) explains why a supplier's percentage tells you little about your gate.

## Limitations

None of this measures how much proxy attendance you had before, and a drop in flags after a change is not proof that the problem has gone. It may have moved to an uncovered gate or to a lenient reviewer. Review the exception log by shift and by reviewer every month. And if your attendance problems are mostly genuine lateness, a written [late-mark policy](/insights/late-mark-policy-design) will do more than new technology.
