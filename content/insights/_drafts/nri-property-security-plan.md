**Straight answer: a camera alert on your phone abroad protects nothing on its own. A remote property security plan names who acts locally when an alert fires, what they should and should not do, who backs them up, and what happens when the power, internet or a camera fails. Remote viewing is for you to verify and coordinate; local intervention is someone else's job, and it has to be agreed before the first 3 am alert, not during it.**

Many Punjabi families abroad own a kothi, a farmhouse or a plot that stays empty for months. The cameras, recorder and phone app usually exist already. Our [NRI property security page](/nri-property-security) covers how alerts from those cameras can reach you abroad. This article is about the step after the alert: who does something, and what happens when the system itself goes quiet.

## Remote viewing and local intervention are two different jobs

When your phone buzzes, you can do three things from abroad: look at the live view, look at the recording, and phone someone. That is all. You cannot open a gate, confront anyone, or check whether a door was forced.

| What you can do remotely | What needs someone on the ground |
|---|---|
| Confirm whether an alert shows a person, an animal or nothing | Check doors, locks and the boundary in person |
| See which camera and what time | Meet the police at the property |
| Decide who to call | Reset a router, restart a recorder, replace a fuse |
| Save or note the clip for later | Clean a lens, re-aim a camera, clear a fallen branch |
| Tell the local contact what you see while they are on the way | Deal with a tenant, a caretaker or a neighbour face to face |

A plan that only covers the left column is a recording system with a phone attached.

## Time zones decide who answers first

An alert at 2 am in Punjab reaches you at a very different hour depending on where you live. India is roughly 9½ to 13½ hours ahead of the main cities in Canada and the US, depending on the city and daylight saving, and 4½ to 5½ hours ahead of the UK. So a 2 am alert in Punjab arrives in your afternoon in Toronto or Vancouver, your evening in London, and in the early morning in Australia. Your local contact, meanwhile, is asleep.

That changes the plan. If you live where Punjab's night is your daytime, you are well placed to verify first and wake the local contact only when it matters. If Punjab's night is also your night, the local contact or caretaker may need to receive alerts directly, so the plan does not depend on you waking up.

## The response-contact plan (template)

Fill this in with your family, share it with everyone named on it, and review it every time someone moves, changes number or stops being available.

### 1. Contact ladder

| Order | Name and relationship | Phone | Distance to the property | Available when | Has keys? | Agreed to the role? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. First local contact | | | | | | |
| 2. Backup local contact | | | | | | |
| 3. Caretaker or watchman | | | | | | |
| 4. Owner abroad | | | n/a | Local hours: | n/a | n/a |
| 5. Police | 112 (emergency), plus the local police station's number | | | 24 hours | n/a | n/a |
| 6. Installer or service technician | | | | | | |

"Agreed to the role" matters. A cousin who once said "call me anytime" has not agreed to drive to a farmhouse at night. Ask directly, and accept a no.

### 2. What each kind of alert means and who acts

| Event | First action | Who | What they must **not** do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Person inside the boundary at night, house empty | Owner or first contact checks the clip; if a person is confirmed, call 112 and the first local contact | Owner / first contact | Do not go in alone or confront anyone; wait for the police |
| Vehicle in the driveway, no visit expected | Check the clip; call the caretaker to ask if a visit was arranged | Owner, then caretaker | Do not assume it is family |
| Expected visitor (caretaker, cleaner, gardener) | Note the time; no action | Owner | Do not create alerts for enrolled regular visitors if your system allows them to be excluded |
| Boundary wall or gate damaged | First local contact visits in daylight and photographs it | First contact | Do not repair evidence away before it is photographed |
| One camera shows nothing | Caretaker checks power and the lens at the next visit | Caretaker, then technician | Do not ignore it for weeks |
| All cameras offline | See the outage plan below | First contact | Do not assume "quiet night" |

Your system may or may not be able to tell a family member from a stranger; that depends on what is set up and tested at your site. Write the plan for the alerts your system actually sends.

### 3. Outage plan

The most dangerous failure is the silent one. When the power or internet goes at the property, the cameras stop sending anything. On your phone, that looks exactly like a quiet night.

| What fails | What usually happens | What to agree |
|---|---|---|
| Grid power | Cameras, recorder and router stop unless on a UPS or inverter | Put the recorder, router and any network switch on a UPS or inverter; agree how long it must hold |
| Internet | Recording may continue locally, but no alerts or live view reach you | Agree a time limit: if you cannot see live view for longer than this, the first contact checks in person |
| Recorder | Nothing is recorded, nothing is sent | A monthly check by the caretaker that the recorder's lights and clock look normal |
| A single camera | A blind spot nobody notices | A weekly look at every camera's live view by you, from abroad |
| Your phone or app | Alerts reach nobody | A second family member who also receives alerts |

How long the "time limit" should be depends on the property. A house in a busy colony with neighbours may tolerate a day. A farmhouse with a tubewell motor and cable worth stealing may need a visit within hours. Decide it and write it down. Our article on [knowing when a camera has gone offline](/insights/cctv-camera-offline-how-to-know) covers how to notice a silent camera sooner.

### 4. Practical arrangements

- [ ] Keys: who holds a set, and where the spare is kept.
- [ ] Documents: a copy of the property papers and a family contact sheet with the first local contact, so they can show the police they are acting for you.
- [ ] Access to footage: who besides you can view and export a clip. Give them their own login with view-only rights, never your admin password.
- [ ] Never send camera passwords, stream addresses or footage over WhatsApp or other messaging groups. If footage is needed for a police complaint, export it from the recorder or app; our [day-after-a-theft checklist](/insights/what-to-do-day-after-a-theft) explains how.
- [ ] Tenants: cameras should cover the gate, boundary and common areas, not the inside of a tenant's home.
- [ ] Visits: agree how often the caretaker or first contact walks the property even when nothing has alerted.

## Test the plan, not just the cameras

Twice a year — and every time you visit — run a short drill:

1. Ask someone on site to walk through the gate at an agreed time after dark.
2. Note when the alert reaches you, and when it reaches anyone else who receives it.
3. Phone the first local contact as if it were real. Note how long they take to answer and how long it would take them to reach the property.
4. Unplug the router for a set period. Note whether anyone notices, and how.
5. Write down what went wrong and fix one thing before you fly back.

The drill takes an hour and tells you more than any brochure.

## When cameras are not the main answer

For some properties the plan works out cheaper and safer without more technology. A vacant plot may need a boundary wall and a monthly visit more than it needs cameras. A house in a close-knit colony may be best protected by a neighbour who keeps an eye on it and a light on a timer. If every person on your contact ladder is hours away, an alert will not bring anyone in time; better locks, removing valuables and an occasional paid watchman may protect more than another camera would.
