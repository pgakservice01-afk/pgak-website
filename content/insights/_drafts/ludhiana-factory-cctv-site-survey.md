**Straight answer: before any installer quotes your factory, walk the site yourself with a supervisor and write down, area by area, what each camera must show, when it must show it, and what conditions it will face. A one-hour survey covering the gates, the boundary, the production floor, stores, dispatch, night lighting and support access gives every installer the same brief — and makes their quotes comparable.**

Most CCTV quotes for factories start from the wrong question: "How many cameras do you want?" The better question is what you need to see, at which spot, at what hour. Only you and your staff know that. Installers know cameras; you know where material leaves, which gate the night shift uses, and which corner of the wall has been crossed before.

Ludhiana's manufacturing units — hosiery and textiles, cycle parts, auto components, machine tools, forging — share some conditions that a survey should look for: fibre and lint in the air, metal dust and oil mist, heat and vibration near presses and furnaces, long compound walls, open plots alongside, and power cuts. The checklist below is built around those.

## How to use this checklist

Print it, take a supervisor who knows the night shift, and walk the site twice: once in daylight, once after dark. At each position write a short answer, not a tick. "Gate 2: need face of anyone walking in, 6 am to 10 pm, faces into morning sun" is useful. "Gate 2: ✓" is not.

Take a phone photo from where you imagine the camera would sit. That photo, with your note, is the most useful thing you can hand an installer.

If you want to work out the camera count separately, our guide on [how many CCTV cameras a factory needs](/insights/how-many-cctv-cameras-does-a-factory-need) explains the events-first method, and [where to place cameras so AI detection works](/insights/where-to-place-cctv-cameras-for-ai-detection) covers mounting height and angle.

## The printable survey

### 1. Site basics

- [ ] Shifts and hours: when is the site fully staffed, partly staffed, empty?
- [ ] Who is on site at night: guard, watchman, nobody?
- [ ] Power: how often does the grid supply fail, and is there a DG set? Which circuits does it carry?
- [ ] Is there a UPS today for the recorder and network switch? For how long does it hold?
- [ ] Where is the existing recorder, if any? Is that room locked, ventilated and dry?
- [ ] Internet: which connection, and is it needed for remote viewing or alerts?
- [ ] Existing cameras: how many, where, and which ones currently show a usable picture?

### 2. Gates

Record each gate separately — vehicle gate, pedestrian gate, any side or rear gate.

- [ ] What must the camera show here: a face, a number plate, a vehicle type, or just "someone passed"?
- [ ] Hours the gate is used, and by whom (staff, contractors, visitors, trucks).
- [ ] Direction of sun in the morning and evening; headlight glare at night.
- [ ] Vehicle speed through the gate and where vehicles stop, if they stop.
- [ ] Guard cabin: is there someone to act on what the camera shows?
- [ ] Is there an existing register (visitor, vehicle, material out) that footage should be matched against?

A camera mounted high to "see the whole gate" usually sees the tops of heads and cannot read a plate. If you need faces or plates, say so here; the installer will need a separate, lower or closer position. The [number plate recognition guide](/insights/anpr-number-plate-recognition-when-it-works) explains the conditions.

### 3. Boundary and perimeter

- [ ] Length of each wall section, and what lies on the other side (road, open plot, neighbouring unit, field).
- [ ] Wall height, and whether there are trees, sheds or stacked material that make climbing easier.
- [ ] Lighting along each section after dark.
- [ ] Known crossing points from past incidents.
- [ ] What counts as an event: anyone crossing at any time, or only outside working hours?
- [ ] Who is told, and how fast, if someone crosses at 2 am?

The last two questions decide whether the boundary is just recorded or actively watched. If you want an alert when a line is crossed, read [how intrusion alerts work](/features/intrusion-alerts) and note that animals, swaying branches and headlights are the usual sources of false alarms along a wall.

### 4. Production floor

- [ ] What must be seen: high-value work in progress, a machine area, a material entry point, general activity?
- [ ] Mounting heights available (roof truss, column, wall) and how a technician would reach them.
- [ ] Dust, lint, oil mist, steam or heat at each position; distance from furnaces, presses and paint or dyeing areas.
- [ ] Vibration: is the proposed mount on a column shared with a press?
- [ ] Areas that must **not** be covered: toilets, changing rooms, rest areas where privacy is expected. See our guide on [CCTV in the workplace](/insights/cctv-workplace-privacy-india).
- [ ] Do workers know where cameras are and why? Is there signage? See [what a CCTV notice should say](/insights/cctv-signage-requirements-india).

### 5. Stores, raw material and scrap

- [ ] Where is value concentrated: finished goods store, raw material, copper or brass, scrap yard?
- [ ] Who has keys, and when is the store opened?
- [ ] Is material stacked outdoors? Is that area lit at night?
- [ ] Is scrap weighed and dispatched on a schedule that footage should be checked against?

### 6. Dispatch and loading

- [ ] Number of bays and where trucks stand while loading.
- [ ] Dispatch hours, and whether loading ever happens outside them.
- [ ] Lighting at the bays after dark.
- [ ] What must be seen: the vehicle number, the count of bundles or sacks, who loaded, the gate exit.
- [ ] Is there a dispatch register or e-way bill record to match footage against?

### 7. Night lighting walk

Do this section after dark, standing at each proposed camera position.

- [ ] Can you see the area clearly with your own eyes? If not, a camera will struggle too.
- [ ] Note dark patches, lights that are switched off at night to save power, and lights that shine straight at a likely camera position.
- [ ] Record a ten-second phone video at each position for the installer.
- [ ] If an existing camera covers the spot, play back last night's recording: can you tell a person from a shadow? Could you recognise someone you know?

Adding a light is often cheaper than upgrading a camera, and it helps the guard as well.

### 8. Support access and installation practicalities

- [ ] Which positions need a ladder, scaffold or lift? Who provides it?
- [ ] Roof access: is it safe, and who must be present?
- [ ] Cable routes: existing trays and conduit, walls that may or may not be drilled, road crossings inside the compound.
- [ ] Hours when installers may work without disrupting production.
- [ ] Who on your side will hold the recorder admin password and approve the handover?
- [ ] Who will a technician call when a camera fails, and who lets them in on a Sunday?

## What to do with the completed survey

Give the same completed sheet and photos to every installer you ask to quote. Ask each of them to respond position by position: the camera type, why, and what it will **not** show. Quotes that ignore your survey and return a round camera count are easy to spot.

Then put two numbers beside the quotes. First, how much alert-checking time an intrusion rule on the boundary would create or save at your site — the [intrusion and line-crossing calculator](/features/intrusion-alerts#scenario-C07) takes your own figures for that. Second, what false alarms currently cost you in call-outs and wasted checks; the [false-alarm cost calculator](/calculators/false-alarm-cost) keeps staff time separate from cash.

## Where this checklist stops

A self-survey does not tell you whether an existing camera's stream is compatible with analytics software, what cable grade a run needs, or how much storage a given resolution will consume. Those are technical checks for whoever quotes. What it does give you is a clear statement of what you need, which is the part most quotes skip.

It also does not decide whether you need anything new. Sometimes the survey shows the cameras are fine and the real gaps are a broken light, a gate left open, or a recorder nobody checks. Fixing those costs less than any camera.
