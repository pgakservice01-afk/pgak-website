**Straight answer: CCTV seldom stops showroom shrinkage while it is happening. What it can do, inside a disciplined workflow, is explain part of a stock discrepancy. First reconcile at cost price and rule out paperwork errors. Narrow the unexplained loss to an item, a place and a time window. Then review only those camera windows, while they are still on the recorder, and log what you found. Track the results month by month. No camera, search tool or analytics package comes with a loss-reduction rate you can count on in advance.**

Most month-end counts end the same way: something is short, nobody saw anything, and the footage exists somewhere. Reviewing three cameras across a whole month is not a job anyone finishes. The fix is not more footage. It is a smaller, better-defined question before anyone opens the recorder.

## Why start with the stock, not the footage?

A short count is not proof of theft. In many showrooms, a large part of any discrepancy turns out to be paperwork:

- an exchange taken back but not restocked or not entered
- an item billed under the wrong code, so one line is short and another is over
- a supplier short-delivery accepted without a check
- damaged stock thrown away but not written off
- a transfer to another branch entered late

Each of these looks like loss until someone checks it. Reviewing footage for a paperwork error wastes hours and can lead to the wrong person being suspected. So the order matters: **reconcile, explain what you can, then investigate what is left.**

## The stock-loss reconciliation worksheet

Value every line at **cost price**, meaning what you paid, not the tag price. Then split the variance into what you can explain and what you cannot.

### Illustrative example: one month at an apparel and accessories showroom

Round, hypothetical figures to show the arithmetic.

| Item group | Book qty | Counted | Variance | Cost per unit | Variance at cost | Explained, and how | Unexplained at cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Formal shoes | 120 | 116 | −4 | ₹1,500 | −₹6,000 | 2 pairs found at the exchange desk, not restocked | −₹3,000 |
| Belts | 200 | 191 | −9 | ₹250 | −₹2,250 | None found | −₹2,250 |
| Kurtas | 300 | 302 | +2 | ₹600 | +₹1,200 | Billed under a similar code; corrected | ₹0 |
| Sunglasses | 80 | 74 | −6 | ₹700 | −₹4,200 | 1 damaged unit thrown away, not written off | −₹3,500 |
| **Total** | | | | | **−₹11,250** | | **−₹8,750** |

**Reading it.** The net variance at cost is −₹6,000 − ₹2,250 + ₹1,200 − ₹4,200 = −₹11,250. After checking exchanges, billing codes and write-offs, ₹8,750 is unexplained. That is what deserves investigation. The kurta surplus is a clue too. When one line is over, look for a matching shortfall in a line that is often billed by mistake in its place.

**Choosing what to investigate first.** Rank unexplained lines by value. Then ask one question: *do we still have footage for the window?* Belts and sunglasses are small, sit near the entrance and the counter, and went missing in larger numbers. They are better first candidates than the shoes, where only two pairs are unexplained.

## Narrow the window before you open the recorder

For each line you investigate, write down:

1. **When it was last right.** The date of the last correct count or the last confirmed sale of that item.
2. **Where it could have been.** Display, trial room exit, exchange desk, stockroom, billing counter.
3. **Which cameras see those places**, and which do not. A blind spot is a finding in its own right.
4. **Whether footage still exists.** Compare the window with your recorder's real retention, not the figure quoted at installation. Our [storage article](/insights/cctv-storage-how-many-days) shows how to work that out.

If the last correct count was a month ago and the recorder keeps 15 days, half of your window is already gone. This is the most common reason investigations fail. The practical fix is to **count high-loss lines weekly**. Belts and sunglasses take minutes to count, and weekly counts keep the window inside your retention.

## The incident review record

Every review gets a record, whether or not it finds anything. It protects the business and the staff, and it is what makes trends visible later.

| Field | Example entry |
|---|---|
| Case number and date opened | S-2026-014, 8 Oct |
| Item and unexplained value at cost | Sunglasses, 5 units, ₹3,500 |
| Window reviewed | 1–7 Oct, 10:00–21:00 |
| Cameras reviewed | Entrance, counter, display wall |
| Reviewer and time spent | Store manager, 1 h 40 min |
| Finding | 2 units seen leaving unbilled on 3 Oct; 3 not accounted for |
| Clips exported, with export record | 2 clips, exported 8 Oct, kept on a separate drive |
| Process gap identified | Display wall partly hidden from the counter |
| Action | Display moved into counter view; weekly count started |
| Closed by and date | Owner, 10 Oct |

Footage is one piece of evidence. A clip that shows someone handling an item does not show intent. Combine it with billing records and rosters, follow your own disciplinary process, and take advice before acting against anyone. If a case may go to the police or an insurer, export the clips properly first. Our [day-after-a-theft checklist](/insights/what-to-do-day-after-a-theft) and the article on [CCTV footage as evidence](/insights/cctv-footage-legal-evidence-india) cover the steps.

## Where do searchable footage and cross-camera search fit?

Search tools reduce the time per case. They do not decide which cases to open. Tools that let you find a person or item across several cameras can be evaluated at your site. They give a reviewer leads to check, not a confirmed identity. Similar uniforms, poor light and gaps between cameras can break the trail. The [cross-camera search guide](/features/guides/cross-camera-search) lists what to test before you rely on one.

Time is the honest way to value them. The [investigation time calculator](/calculators/investigation-time) and the [cross-camera search calculator](/features/guides/cross-camera-search#scenario-C02) both work on hours, not rupees of stock recovered.

*Illustrative example:* 6 cases a month × 4 cameras × 15 minutes per camera gives 60 minutes per case today. Compare that with a searched review of 12 minutes plus 3 minutes of checking. That is 6 × (60 − 12 − 3) ÷ 60 = **4.5 hours a month**. The search minutes are yours to measure in a trial, not a supplier's figure.

The [shrinkage calculator](/calculators/shrinkage-reduction) deliberately has no default recovery rate. Leave it alone until you have at least three months of reconciliations recorded this way. Then you can enter a change you actually measured.

## When does this workflow not justify new tools?

- **Low stock value and two or three cameras.** A manual review of the narrowed window is quick enough without search tools.
- **Most of the variance is explained.** Fix receiving, exchanges and billing codes first. Those fixes cost nothing.
- **Basic controls are missing.** A second person at cash reconciliation, receipts required for returns, and a locked stockroom do more than any camera.
