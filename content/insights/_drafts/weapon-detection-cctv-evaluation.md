**Straight answer: weapon detection analytics watch for a weapon held in a camera's view and raise an alert so a person can look and decide. It cannot see anything concealed, it will sometimes flag ordinary objects, and its value depends entirely on the response plan behind it. Before buying, ask where the model came from, how it was tested, what test material you are allowed to use, and exactly what happens when it is wrong.**

This is a capability where the cost of a mistake runs both ways. A missed alert gives false comfort. A false alert, handled badly, can mean a guard confronting an innocent visitor. That is why the evaluation matters more than the demonstration.

PGAK has no published evidence for weapon detection. Where it is part of a proposal, it is a licensed third-party module, evaluated at the specific site. Nothing in this article is a claim that it works at yours. The [weapon detection guide](/features/guides/weapon-detection) explains the capability in plain terms.

## What can it do, and what can it not?

| It can | It cannot |
|---|---|
| Flag a weapon-like object held in clear view of a camera | See anything inside a bag, under clothing or out of view |
| Send an alert to a person with a clip | Decide whether a person is a threat |
| Shorten the time before someone looks | Stop anyone, lock a door safely, or replace a guard |
| Work on cameras it has been tested on | Promise the same result on a different camera, angle or light |

A tool held at waist height in a crowded frame is a much harder case than a clear demonstration. You should see both before you buy.

## The buyer's evaluation questionnaire

Send these questions to every supplier and ask for written answers. A supplier who cannot answer a section has told you something useful. This questionnaire is the original asset of the article.

### Section A: Where the model came from

1. Who developed the detection model, and who owns it? If it is licensed, name the licensor.
2. Which version will be installed, and how are updates delivered and tested before they reach your site?
3. What object classes does it detect? Name them. "Weapons" is not a class list.
4. What kind of footage was it trained on: indoor, outdoor, Indian sites, camera heights similar to yours?
5. Has it been tested independently? If so, by whom, under what conditions, and can you see the report with its denominators: how many real events, how many missed, how many false alerts?
6. Which camera models, resolutions and mounting heights does the supplier support for this module?

### Section B: What test material is permitted

7. What test method does the supplier propose for your site?
8. Will the test use only props or replicas that your security head has approved? Real weapons should never be brought on site for a camera test.
9. Has the test been planned so that nobody outside the test can see a prop and mistake it for a real threat? For any public-facing area, contact the local police before staging anything.

10. Will the test include ordinary objects that look similar in shape, such as drills, pipes, umbrellas, phones held at an angle and tools from your own site, to measure false alerts?
11. Will it run at your real camera heights, distances and lighting, by day and by night, and with people in the frame?
12. Who keeps the test footage, for how long, and is it deleted after the evaluation?

### Section C: False alerts and escalation

13. How many false alerts per camera per day should you expect on a normal working day, and how will that be measured during the trial rather than estimated?
14. What does the alert contain: a still, a clip, the camera name, the time?
15. Who receives the alert on each shift, and on what device?
16. What must that person check in the video before doing anything else?
17. What are the next steps if the alert looks real: who is called, and in what order? Calling the police is a human decision, never an automatic one.
18. What are the next steps if it looks false: how is it closed and recorded, and who reviews false alerts weekly?
19. What instruction will guards receive so that an alert never leads to confronting a person on the strength of the software alone?
20. What happens if the processing unit, the network or the alert route fails? How will anyone know?

### Section D: Data, people and contract

21. Is any footage sent off site for processing? Where, and who can access it?
22. Who can view alert clips, and is that access logged? See [CCTV in the workplace](/insights/cctv-workplace-privacy-india) for the wider privacy context.
23. How long are alert clips kept, and who deletes them?
24. What does the contract say about the module's licence term, update support and what happens if the licensor withdraws it?
25. Will the supplier state in writing that this is not a screening or scanning system?

## How to score the answers

Do not average a score. Instead, mark each section:

- **Answered with evidence**: documents, test reports with denominators, named people.
- **Answered in words only**: a reasonable plan, nothing to check.
- **Not answered.**

Any "not answered" in Section C means the system is not ready for your site, whatever it does in a demonstration. Without an escalation plan, a weapon alert is a liability, not a safeguard.

### Illustrative example

These numbers are invented and round, to show the arithmetic only.

A two-week on-site trial runs on 3 entrance cameras.

- Staged prop events: 30, across day and night. Alerts raised: 21. Missed: 30 − 21 = 9, or 9 ÷ 30 = 30%. Each miss is reviewed with the supplier to see whether camera position or lighting was the cause.
- False alerts in normal operation: 42 over 14 days on 3 cameras = 42 ÷ (14 × 3) = 1 per camera per day, mostly from tools carried by maintenance staff.

That means a guard would check about one false alert per entrance per day, and that nearly a third of clear test events were missed. Whether that is worth having is your decision, but it is now a decision made on your own figures, not a brochure.

## When is weapon detection the wrong purchase?

- **When there is no one to respond.** An alert at an unmanned gate is a recording, not a safeguard.
- **When the real need is screening.** If you must know what people carry in, that is a screening process with trained staff and suitable equipment, not CCTV analytics.
- **When cameras do not see entrances at person height.** Cameras mounted high for wide coverage rarely give a usable view of an object in a hand.
- **When false alerts would be handled by confrontation.** Fix the response plan first.
