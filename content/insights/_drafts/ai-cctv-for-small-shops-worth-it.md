**Straight answer: for many small shops, AI CCTV is not worth buying. If the owner sits at the counter all day, the stock is low in value and the measured monthly loss is small, the full monthly cost of analytics can exceed everything it could possibly save — and cheaper fixes such as re-aiming the till camera, moving high-value items behind the counter and a weekly stock check fit better. The arithmetic changes only with high-value stock, more than one branch, or a real after-hours risk.**

An AI analytics system is a real monthly cost for a small business, and it deserves a real answer rather than a pitch. Below is the break-even arithmetic, with an example where it fails, followed by the cases where it can work.

## What "worth it" has to mean

A system is worth buying when what it saves, over its life, is more than what it costs. Three things get mixed up here, and they should be kept apart:

- **Cash saved** — money you would otherwise lose or spend: stock that stops disappearing, a guard you would otherwise hire, a call-out fee you stop paying.
- **Time freed** — hours you no longer spend scrolling footage or checking the shop at night. Valuable, but not cash unless you use those hours to earn or stop paying for something.
- **Risk reduced** — the chance of a large one-off loss, such as a break-in. Real, but hard to measure, and easy to inflate.

A fair break-even test uses cash saved against cost, and lists time and risk beside it rather than adding them in.

## A break-even example where it does not pay

*Illustrative example. The shop, the quote and every figure are hypothetical, rounded to show the arithmetic. The quote is not a PGAK price; replace it with the figures in any written proposal you receive.*

**The shop.** A general store with four cameras on an existing DVR. The owner is at the counter from opening to closing. The most expensive items on the shelves cost a few hundred rupees each. Monthly stock counts show about **₹2,000** of unexplained loss, at cost price.

**The quote.** ₹60,000 one-time for processing hardware and setup, plus ₹2,500 a month for software and support, on a three-year term.

**Monthly cost over three years:**

- One-time cost spread over 36 months: ₹60,000 ÷ 36 = ₹1,667
- Monthly software and support: ₹2,500
- **Total: about ₹4,167 a month**

**Best case for cash saved.** Even if the system eliminated all ₹2,000 of loss — which no camera system does, because an alert does not put stock back on the shelf — the shop would lose about ₹2,167 a month.

**A more realistic case.** Suppose it helped cut the loss by half: ₹1,000 a month saved. The shop is then about **₹3,167 a month worse off**, or roughly ₹1,14,000 over three years.

**Break-even point.** For a system that cuts loss by half to pay for itself at this cost, the shop would need to be losing about ₹8,334 a month (₹4,167 × 2) — over four times what it actually loses.

**Time freed.** Close to none. The owner already sees every customer and the till.

**Risk reduced.** An after-hours alert might help if someone broke in. But with low-value stock and a shutter, the largest likely loss is small, and an alert at 2 am still needs someone to respond.

**People counting.** It adds little here. The owner can see every person who walks in. Counting visitors only helps when you cannot see the door yourself.

**Verdict:** in this example, AI CCTV does not pay.

## The simpler option that fits this shop

For the same shop, a set of low-cost changes addresses the same problem directly:

| Fix | What it addresses | Illustrative one-time cost |
|---|---|---|
| Re-aim the till camera so it shows the cash drawer and the counter top | Disputes over cash and over-the-counter items | A technician visit, say ₹1,000 |
| Move the highest-value, easiest-to-pocket items behind or beside the counter | Small, repeated pilferage | Nil |
| A convex mirror for the one aisle the counter cannot see | The blind aisle | Say ₹1,500 |
| Check the DVR's real retention and set its built-in motion recording for after hours | Footage still being there when you need it | Nil to a small fee |
| A weekly spot count of the twenty items that go missing most | Finding out *what* is disappearing and when | Owner time |
| A daily cash-up at shift change if more than one person handles the till | Cash discrepancies | Staff time |

Total: about **₹2,500 once**, against about ₹4,167 a month for the analytics quote. If the losses fall, you have your answer. If they do not, the spot counts will tell you which items and which hours, which is exactly the information you would need before buying anything more. Our guide to [using footage as an evidence index for shrinkage](/insights/showroom-retail-shrinkage) explains how to search what you already record.

## When the arithmetic changes

The same test can come out the other way. These are the situations where it is worth running properly:

**High-value stock.** A mobile phone shop, a jewellery counter or a pharmacy with costly medicines can lose more in one incident than a general store loses in a year. The break-even point is reached faster. See our guide to [jewellery shop security](/insights/jewellery-shop-security-india) for why layers matter more than camera count there.

**More than one branch.** If you own two or three shops and can stand in only one, the time freed is real: fewer trips, and fewer evenings checking footage. Count that time separately from cash, but it can decide the question.

**A real after-hours risk.** Stock worth lakhs behind a shutter on an empty street, with someone who can respond to an alert, is a different case from a small store with a watchman next door.

**Staff on the floor and footfall you cannot see.** In a larger shop with several staff and more than one entrance, comparing visitor counts with bills can show whether busy hours are being lost. The [people counting calculator](/features/guides/people-counting#scenario-C11) estimates the time involved with your own figures, and the [retail contribution calculator](/calculators/retail-contribution) shows what a change in conversion would be worth in margin, not just sales.

## The borderline case: no known loss, but uneasy

If you have no measured loss and simply feel uneasy, sit with that before spending. Security bought against an unquantified worry is often regretted. Start by measuring: a month of spot counts and a check that your existing cameras actually see the till and the door. If you later decide analytics is justified, start with the two or three cameras that cover what you would actually mind losing, not the whole shop, and make sure any proposal shows the cost for that smaller scope.

## Before you buy anything

Check three things first:

1. **Can your cameras see what matters?** If the till camera shows the top of the owner's head and the aisle camera faces the light, analytics will not help until that is fixed.
2. **What is your real monthly loss?** From stock counts and cash-ups, not a guess.
3. **What would the full monthly cost be?** Including any hardware and setup spread over the term.

If the third number is larger than what you could realistically save from the second, the honest answer is not yet. Our page on [retail shop security](/retail-shop-security) covers the wider picture.
