**Straight answer: to use CCTV near-miss clips safely in an EHS review, export only the seconds that matter, store them in one restricted place, de-identify people where the lesson does not depend on who they are, and review them for causes and corrective actions rather than blame. Write down who can see each clip and when it will be deleted. The template below does that in one page.**

A near miss on video is one of the most useful things a safety team can have. A forklift and a pedestrian at a blind corner, a load swinging over a walkway, a reversing lorry and a porter: watched together, the clip ends arguments about what happened and points straight at the layout or procedure that needs changing. It also shows real people, often recognisable, at a moment they may not want replayed. Handled carelessly, the clip ends up on a WhatsApp group and the lesson is lost in the embarrassment.

## What are the decision variables?

**Purpose.** A near-miss review is about prevention: what in the layout, equipment, procedure or workload made this possible? Write that purpose on the review record. It shapes everything else.

**Who sees the clip.** The fewest people who can fix the cause: usually the EHS lead, the area supervisor and whoever owns the corrective action. A safety committee may see a de-identified version.

**What is exported.** The relevant seconds, not the hour. A 40-second clip from one camera is easier to protect and easier to learn from.

**Identification.** Does the lesson depend on who was involved? Usually not. Faces, and sometimes uniforms with names, can be blurred.

**Retention.** How long the clip is kept, by whom, and when it is deleted.

## What about the law?

The Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020 was made effective from 21 November 2025 (Press Information Bureau, Ministry of Labour & Employment, "Government Makes the Four Labour Codes effective to Simplify and Streamline Labour Laws", 21 November 2025). Under it, establishments with 500 or more workers will form safety committees with employer and worker representation (PIB backgrounder, "India's Labour Reforms: Simplification, Security, and Sustainable Growth", 21 November 2025). Where you have one, it is a natural forum for de-identified near-miss reviews.

An internal near-miss review does not replace any statutory duty to report an accident or dangerous occurrence. ## The restricted-access review template

One record per near miss. This template is the original asset of this article.

### Part A: The event

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| Review reference | NM-____ |
| Date and time of event | |
| Location and camera(s) | |
| How it came to notice | Reported by a person / spotted on review / analytics alert |
| Short neutral description | What happened, without names. Example: "Forklift reversing from aisle 4 passed within an arm's length of a pedestrian crossing the walkway." |

### Part B: The clip

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| Clip exported by | Name and role |
| Start and end time exported | Only the seconds needed |
| Stored at | One restricted folder, not email or messaging apps |
| Original recording preserved? | Yes / No, and where, if it may be needed later |
| De-identified version made? | Yes / No; method: faces blurred, names on uniforms blurred |
| Who may view the original | Named roles only |
| Who may view the de-identified version | E.g. safety committee, area team briefing |
| Delete original on | Date |
| Deleted on, by | Date, name |

### Part C: The review

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| What sequence of events does the clip show? | |
| What made this possible: layout, visibility, equipment, procedure, workload, training? | |
| Has this, or something like it, happened before? | |
| What does the clip not show? Off-camera causes, sound, what people could see from where they stood | |
| Who was spoken to, and what did they add? | |

### Part D: Corrective actions

| Action | Type | Owner | Due | Done | Checked effective? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Example: mirror at aisle 4 corner | Engineering | Maintenance head | | | |
| Example: walkway barrier extended 3 m | Engineering | Maintenance head | | | |
| Example: reversing rule briefed to all drivers | Procedure | Warehouse supervisor | | | |
| Example: watch same camera for 2 weeks after change | Verification | EHS lead | | | |

Prefer actions that change the workplace (barriers, mirrors, layout, lighting) over actions that only tell people to be careful.

## How to de-identify a clip

- **Blur faces** of everyone not essential to the lesson, which is usually everyone.
- **Blur name tags, employee numbers and distinctive uniforms** where they would identify someone to colleagues.
- **Trim the clip** to the event; earlier and later footage often shows people arriving or leaving and adds nothing.
- **Remove audio**, if the camera recorded any.
- **Check the result** by asking: would someone in the area team know who this is? If yes, and it does not matter to the lesson, blur more.

Some platforms offer automatic face masking; it can be evaluated at your site, but test it on side profiles and people at the edge of the frame, and check the result by eye. The [privacy masking guide](/features/guides/privacy-masking) explains what to ask.

## Where can analytics and summaries help?

Two places. First, finding near misses nobody reported: a forklift-and-pedestrian rule in a marked zone can flag candidates for review. Second, writing up: an AI event summary can draft a neutral description of the clip for Part A, which the reviewer then checks against the video. The summary organises the review; the recording remains the evidence. The [event summaries guide](/features/guides/event-summaries) explains the limits. Neither has a PGAK evidence record; both can be evaluated at your site.

### Illustrative example

These numbers are invented and round, to show the arithmetic only.

An EHS lead writes up 20 near-miss reviews a month. Finding and exporting the right clip, and writing Part A by hand, takes 30 minutes per review. With a drafted summary that is then checked against the video, it takes 18 minutes.

- Minutes saved per review: 30 − 18 = 12.
- Hours saved per month: 20 × 12 ÷ 60 = 4 hours.

That time is better spent on Part D. It is review effort only; no value is put on injuries avoided, and no claim is made that any injury is prevented.

## What this workflow should never become

- **A blame file.** If workers believe clips are collected to punish them, near misses stop being reported.
- **Automatic discipline.** No analytics flag should trigger a penalty, a deduction or a warning.
- **A messaging-group clip.** Never circulate footage on WhatsApp or similar; share it in the meeting, from the restricted folder.
- **A substitute for supervision.** Video review comes after the event. It does not replace supervisors on the floor.
