**Straight answer: measure AI CCTV value after 30 days by comparing a baseline you recorded before the pilot with the same measures during it, in three separate columns — hours freed, cash actually saved, and risk changes you cannot yet prove. Thirty days is enough to measure alert volume, false alerts and review time. It is rarely enough to prove that thefts or break-ins went down, so that column should be written up, not converted into rupees.**

Most AI CCTV "ROI" figures mix three different things into one number: staff time multiplied by a salary, money that genuinely stopped being spent, and losses that supposedly did not happen. Added together, they make almost any system look profitable. Kept apart, they tell you what you actually got.

## Why the three columns must stay separate

**Hours freed** are minutes your people no longer spend checking false alerts, searching footage or patrolling in response to noise. You can measure these well in 30 days. They are not cash: a salaried supervisor who spends less time on alerts costs the same at month end.

**Cash saved** is money that stopped leaving the business, or stayed in it, and that you can point to: paid overtime that ended, a security agency call-out fee no longer charged, a contractor no longer needed. It belongs in a payback calculation. It is often smaller than people expect.

**Risk reduction** is the change in the chance of a large loss — a break-in, a stock theft, an incident at the boundary. It is real but rare, and 30 days is far too short to measure it. A month with no theft after the pilot started does not show the system prevented one; there may simply not have been an attempt.

## Before the pilot: record a baseline

You cannot measure a change without a "before". Record at least two weeks of baseline, ideally a full month, in a normal period — not a festival rush or a shutdown.

| Measure | How to record it |
|---|---|
| Alerts per day or night from the current system | Count from the recorder or app log |
| Minutes to check one alert | Time ten checks and take the average |
| Alerts that needed someone to go and look | Guard or supervisor log |
| Security agency or guard call-outs | Invoices or the guard register |
| Footage searches and time spent | A simple log: what was searched, how long it took |
| Paid overtime linked to monitoring or checks | Payroll |
| Recorded losses or incidents | Stock counts, incident register, police complaints |
| Cameras offline or not recording | A weekly check of every camera |

Agree the event definitions for the pilot before it starts — for example, "a person inside the rear yard between 10 pm and 6 am" — and how true, false and missed alerts will be counted. Our [pilot evaluation worksheet](/resources/evaluation-method) sets out that method in full.

## During the pilot: keep the same log

Record the same measures, the same way, for 30 days, plus four things the new system adds:

- **True alerts:** the agreed event happened.
- **False alerts:** an alert, but the event did not happen.
- **Missed events:** the event happened (from the guard log, another camera or a deliberate test) and no usable alert arrived.
- **Delivery and outages:** how long alerts took to reach the named phone, and any period when cameras, processing or internet were down.

Do not change thresholds or zones halfway through and then compare the whole month as one period. If you tune the system in week two, report weeks one and three-to-four separately.

## The 30-day value ledger (template)

| Line | Baseline (per month) | Pilot (30 days) | Difference | Column |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alerts to check | | | | — |
| Minutes per check | | | | — |
| **Time spent checking alerts (hours)** | | | | Hours freed |
| **Footage search time (hours)** | | | | Hours freed |
| Paid overtime for monitoring (₹) | | | | Cash saved — only if it actually stopped |
| Call-out or agency charges (₹) | | | | Cash saved — only if invoiced less |
| Recorded losses (₹) | | | | Note only — see below |
| True alerts | n/a | | | Evidence for risk column |
| False alerts | n/a | | | Quality measure |
| Missed events found | n/a | | | Quality measure |
| Outage hours | | | | Quality measure |
| **Risk changes observed** | | | | Risk — written description, no ₹ |

Fill the cash column only with money you can show on a payslip, an invoice or a bank statement. Leave recorded losses as a note: with one month on each side, a change in losses is more likely to be chance than proof.

## A worked example

*Illustrative example. The site and every figure are hypothetical, rounded to show the arithmetic. They are not a PGAK customer result and imply no accuracy or return.*

A warehouse uses its recorder's built-in motion alerts on six outdoor cameras. A supervisor checks them each night.

**Baseline (one month):**

- 40 motion alerts a night × 2 minutes each = 80 minutes a night → **40 hours a month** checking alerts
- 10 of those hours fall outside the supervisor's shift and are paid as overtime at ₹200 an hour → **₹2,000 a month**
- 2 security agency call-outs at ₹1,000 each → **₹2,000**
- 3 footage searches after stock queries, about 3 hours each → **9 hours**

**Pilot (30 days), person-and-vehicle alerts on the same six cameras:**

- 6 alerts a night × 2 minutes = 12 minutes a night → **6 hours a month**
- Overtime for alert checks stopped → ₹0
- 1 agency call-out → ₹1,000
- 3 footage searches, now about 1 hour each using event search → **3 hours**
- Of 180 alerts, 150 were the agreed event and 30 were false. 2 missed events found: a person behind stacked pallets, and one during a 3-hour internet outage.

**The ledger:**

| Column | Result |
|---|---|
| Hours freed | (40 − 6) + (9 − 3) = **40 hours a month** |
| Cash saved | Overtime ₹2,000 (stopped) + call-outs ₹1,000 (one fewer, but one month is too short to rely on) = **₹2,000 firm, ₹1,000 tentative** |
| Risk | No theft in either month. One true night alert led to a contractor being stopped at the rear wall; whether that prevented a loss is unknown. **Not monetised.** |
| Quality | 30 false alerts; 2 missed events, one caused by placement and one by an outage |

The honest conclusion: the pilot freed about 40 hours a month and saved about ₹2,000 a month in firm cash. The payback calculation uses ₹2,000, not ₹2,000 plus 40 hours times a salary plus an imagined prevented theft. Whether that justifies the cost depends on the quote. The missed events give two specific fixes — re-aim one camera, and agree what happens when the internet is down — before any rollout.

## Common mistakes that inflate the result

- **Hours × salary = savings.** Only true if the hours are bought back as cash or used to earn.
- **"Thefts prevented."** Without a baseline of attempts, a month without theft proves nothing.
- **Choosing the threshold after seeing the results.** Agree it before the pilot.
- **Comparing unlike months.** A festival month against a quiet one.
- **Ignoring outages.** Hours when the system was down are hours it protected nothing; count them.
- **Counting the old system's worst week.** Use a normal period for the baseline.

## When the 30-day review says stop

Sometimes the ledger shows few hours freed, no cash saved and several missed events. That is a useful result. It may mean the cameras are in the wrong places, the use case was not the real problem, or the site's alert volume was never large enough to matter. Our explainers on [why alerts get muted](/insights/ai-cctv-false-alarms-how-to-reduce) and [why a thousand alerts can hide one real one](/insights/1000-alerts-only-one-is-real) help diagnose which. Stopping after 30 days, or revising and testing again, is cheaper than rolling out something that does not fit.
