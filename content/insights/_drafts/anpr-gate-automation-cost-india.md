**Straight answer: an ANPR gate quote bundles two different purchases — recognition (the plate camera, the software or licence, and the processing that runs it) and automation (the barrier, its safety sensors, controller, civil work and electrics). Quotes that look far apart are usually covering different scope, not charging different prices for the same thing. List every line separately, and you can compare them; and you may find you only need the recognition half.**

The question buyers type is "ANPR camera price". The useful question is "price of what?" A factory that wants a searchable vehicle log needs a fraction of what a society that wants the barrier to lift itself for residents needs. This article gives you a bill of scope you can hand to every supplier.

## Recognition and automation are separate jobs

**Recognition** reads the plate, stores it with a time and snapshot, and lets you search it later or match it against a list. It needs a camera placed for plates, processing to run the reading, software (or a licence for a recognition engine), and a screen for the guard. It does not need to move anything.

**Automation** acts on the result — lifting a barrier for an allow-listed vehicle. That adds a barrier (or a controller on an existing one), safety devices, wiring between the recognition system and the barrier, and a decision about what happens when the read is wrong or the power fails.

Many sites get most of the value from recognition alone: the guard still presses the button, but the register writes itself and becomes searchable. PGAK's photographed gate console works this way — each read is a card the operator approves or denies. Automation can come later, once read quality has been measured on your own gate.

## The bill of scope

Ask every supplier to fill this in. Any blank line is a cost that will appear later, from them or from someone else.

| # | Line item | Recognition only | Recognition + barrier | Questions to settle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Plate camera, one per lane | Yes | Yes | Existing camera reused, repositioned or new? Lens and mounting height? |
| 2 | Night illumination for the plate | Usually | Usually | Built into the camera or separate? Tested against headlight glare? |
| 3 | Mounting, pole or bracket | Yes | Yes | Existing pillar used, or new pole and foundation? |
| 4 | Cabling and power to the camera | Yes | Yes | Cable run length, conduit, surge protection |
| 5 | Processing hardware | Yes | Yes | Sized per lane? On site? Who replaces it if it fails? |
| 6 | Recognition software or licence | Yes | Yes | One-time or recurring? Per lane, per camera or per site? |
| 7 | Operator console and user accounts | Yes | Yes | Who can search, export and edit? |
| 8 | Network and integration | Sometimes | Sometimes | Links to the recorder, ERP or visitor system? Who does it? |
| 9 | Boom barrier or controller | No | Yes | New barrier, or interface to the one you have? |
| 10 | Barrier safety devices | No | Yes | Which certified devices: vehicle presence under the arm, obstruction sensing? |
| 11 | Barrier civil work and electrics | No | Yes | Foundation, power supply, earthing, cable trench |
| 12 | Interface from recognition to barrier | No | Yes | Dry contact, controller, or software link? Who tests it? |
| 13 | Manual override and power-failure mode | No | Yes | How does the arm open in an outage? Who holds the key? |
| 14 | Installation and commissioning | Yes | Yes | Includes a read test on your gate? Written handover? |
| 15 | Annual maintenance and support | Yes | Yes | Camera cleaning, barrier servicing, software updates, response terms |

Lines 9 to 13 are where quotes diverge most, and where responsibility gets blurred if two suppliers are involved. Decide in writing who owns the barrier's safety.

## Barrier safety is not optional scope

A barrier arm that moves on its own near vehicles, motorcyclists and pedestrians can injure someone. Whoever supplies and installs the barrier should:

- fit **certified safety devices** suited to the lane — typically vehicle-presence detection so the arm cannot close on a vehicle beneath it, and obstruction sensing for people and two-wheelers;
- **commission and test** them on site and hand over a written record;
- state the **manual override** and what the arm does on power loss;
- take **responsibility** for safe installation and maintenance in the contract.

Recognition software should never be the only thing deciding when an arm closes. Ask the barrier supplier which certification their safety devices carry, and keep that document with the handover.

## Illustrative example: putting two quotes on the same footing

**Illustrative example (hypothetical quotes, no real prices).** A two-lane factory gate receives two quotes.

- **Quote A** covers lines 1–7 and 14 for both lanes.
- **Quote B** covers lines 1–14 for one lane only, plus line 15 for a year.

They cannot be compared as they stand. To normalise:

1. Mark which lines each covers, lane by lane. Quote B is one lane short for lines 1–14.
2. Ask B for its second-lane price, or ask A whether it can quote lines 9–13 — or get them from a barrier supplier.
3. Ask A to price line 15 so both cover a year of support.
4. Compare: (A + barrier scope + support) against (B + second lane).

Only after step 4 is the difference between quotes a difference in price rather than in scope. If you only need a searchable log, compare A against B's lines 1–7 and 14 and leave the barrier for later.

## What drives the recognition cost up or down

- **Lane count.** Plan one camera per lane; a single camera across two lanes reads one of them poorly. Processing is sized per lane too, so a four-lane gate is not "one ANPR".
- **Whether existing cameras qualify.** A gate camera mounted high for general coverage will usually need repositioning or a dedicated plate camera.
- **Night conditions.** A gate with poor lighting needs illumination planned for plates.
- **What it connects to.** A standalone log is simpler than one linked to your ERP, weighbridge or visitor system.

## When not to automate

Do not automate a barrier before you know your read quality on your own gate, by day and by night. A wrong read on an allow-list either opens for the wrong vehicle or holds a resident at the gate. Run a pilot in recognition-only mode first, measure it, then decide — see [when number-plate recognition works](/insights/anpr-number-plate-recognition-when-it-works) for a test protocol. If your gate is an open yard with no controlled lane, spend first on the lane.
